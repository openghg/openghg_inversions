Boundary-condition operators#

Boundary conditions (BCs) contribute a transported concentration to each observation. First, we define the transport and basis operators for standard multiplicative BCs. Then we describe numerical centering and additive BC corrections in separate sections, before showing the current preparation calculations in pseudocode.

These operators apply to any concentration field. Numerical accuracy needs particular care when the required absolute accuracy is small relative to the background concentration. For example, resolving ppm or smaller changes in O2 near 200,000 ppm requires retaining many significant digits. We explain both the effect of quantized footprint weights and the order of arithmetic operations below. See Surface CO₂/O₂ preparation pilot for the preparation workflow and Configure CO₂ boundary corrections for supported runners and prior configuration.

Transport, concentrations and the boundary basis#

We distinguish boundary grid cells and native times from the states inferred by the model. A state may cover many cells and times, such as one curtain region over one month. Its coefficient is shared across those cells even when their supplied concentrations differ.

We use \(i=1,\ldots,N\) for observations, \(\ell=1,\ldots,L\) for boundary cells at native times, and \(j=1,\ldots,K\) for model states. The native field contains concentrations on the boundary grid before observation averaging. Transport maps those concentrations to observations. Time selection, unit conversion and observation averaging must be consistent between the operators below.

Symbol

Shape and units

Meaning

\(P\)

\(N\times L\), dimensionless

Transport weights derived from particle_locations_n/e/s/w, including time selection and observation averaging.

\(C^0\)

\(L\), concentration

Supplied concentration at each boundary cell and native time.

\(B\)

\(L\times K\), dimensionless

Basis mapping model-state coefficients to the native boundary grid.

\(H_{bc}\)

\(N\times K\), concentration

Transported supplied concentrations weighted by the basis.

\(M\)

\(N\times K\), dimensionless

Transported basis functions without concentration factors.

The basis may contain binary or fractional values. A partition basis is the binary special case: \(B_{\ell j}=1\) when cell and time \(\ell\) belongs to state \(j\), and zero otherwise. Each row has exactly one nonzero entry. For example, (north, January) and (north, February) are distinct states. A fractional basis can instead assign weights 0.25 and 0.75 from two neighbouring state coefficients to a cell. We call any basis satisfying \(B\mathbf1=\mathbf1\) a partition of unity; this includes both examples and preserves constant fields. Defining the field from coefficients and averaging concentrations into observation bins are separate operations.

The built-in north/east/south/west (NESW) basis and complete partitions into disjoint regions partition unity. The current binary boundary-period expansion preserves this property: each observation belongs to exactly one fitted period.

We aim to choose independent native basis columns, so that \(\operatorname{rank}(B)=K\). Reducing redundant columns requires re-expressing the intended prior consistently, preserving its distribution over native fields. Even with independent columns, transport can give \(\operatorname{rank}(PB)<K\): distinct native fields may be indistinguishable to the observing network. Native basis independence and observation-space identifiability are separate scientific checks; the current projection code does not impose a rank check.

Standard multiplicative boundary conditions#

For dimensionless state coefficients \(b\), the native boundary field is \(\operatorname{diag}(C^0)Bb\). Its contribution to observations is

\[\begin{split}H_{bc} &= P\operatorname{diag}(C^0)B, \\ \mu_{bc} &= H_{bc}b, \\ H_{bc,ij} &= \sum_\ell P_{i\ell}C^0_\ell B_{\ell j}.\end{split}\]

For these partition-of-unity bases, \(b=\mathbf1\) gives the supplied native field and \(H_{bc}\mathbf1=PC^0\).

We also define the transported basis, or mass operator,

\[M=PB,\qquad M_{ij}=\sum_\ell P_{i\ell}B_{\ell j}.\]

The code name H_bc_mass refers to transport weight. For example,

\[\begin{split}M=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.2&0.1\\0.1&0.3&0.6\end{pmatrix}\end{split}\]

means that a unit concentration assigned through the first basis state gives responses 0.7 and 0.1 at the two observations. For normalized, complete transport and a basis that partitions unity, \(M\mathbf1=\mathbf1\). We retain the actual vector \(M\mathbf1\) in the centering equations.

Numerical centering#

We choose a scalar reference concentration \(c\) in the same units as \(C^0\) and form the anomaly operator

\[\begin{split}A &= P\operatorname{diag}(C^0-c\mathbf1)B, \\ A_{ij} &= \sum_\ell P_{i\ell}(C^0_\ell-c)B_{\ell j}, \\ H_{bc} &= A+cM.\end{split}\]

H_bc_anomaly stores \(A\), H_bc_mass stores \(M\), and bc_reference stores the provided \(c\). The caller supplies the reference; preparation does not estimate it from the boundary field.

Quantized transport weights and operation order#

Quantization of particle_locations when footprints are saved can change the sum of the stored weights. For a constant 200,000 ppm field and a basis that partitions unity, a total-weight error of \(10^{-4}\) gives a 20 ppm error in the transported baseline. This error comes from the stored weights’ quantization, rather than ordinary float64 rounding during the subsequent calculation.

The native producer checks that the total over all four curtains is finite, positive and within an explicitly justified tolerance of one, then normalizes them together. This corrects the total-weight defect under the assumption of complete boundary coverage. It does not reconstruct individual weights before quantization or correct other transport errors. Totals outside the supplied tolerance are rejected.

After this normalization, we calculate each native anomaly as mass * (concentration - reference) before summation and observation averaging. Choosing \(c\) near the background keeps those native departures small. This ordering evaluates differences before the transport sums and avoids constructing \(A\) by subtracting two accumulated background terms. We use float64 to limit further rounding; it cannot recover precision lost from input concentrations before preparation.

Centered observations and model means#

We choose the fixed observation-space shift

\[r=cM\mathbf1.\]

For the same multiplicative coefficients \(b\),

\[\mu_{bc}-r=Ab+cM(b-\mathbf1).\]

We subtract \(r\) from observations and the total model mean. The likelihood residual is unchanged algebraically. This calculation retains the original BC coefficients and their prior, while evaluating departures around the chosen reference. We restore \(r\) once for absolute predictions. The identity holds whether or not \(M\mathbf1=\mathbf1\).

Normalization addresses the total defect in stored transport weights; forming native anomalies and centering the likelihood address the subsequent arithmetic. The required absolute accuracy relative to the background motivates these steps. Changing the concentration unit changes both numeric values and the required absolute tolerance by the same factor.

Additive boundary corrections#

An additive formulation keeps the projected baseline \(H_{bc}\mathbf1\) fixed and adds state corrections \(d\) in concentration units through native dimensionless transport \(M=PB\). Because the basis partitions unity, the corrected field is \(C=C^0+Bd\). With the example \(M\) above, a 1 ppm correction to the first state changes the first prediction by 0.7 ppm. For NESW, each state is one curtain and fitted period; the prior for these corrections is described below.

The supported additive interfaces require native centered operands H_bc_anomaly, H_bc_mass and scalar bc_reference. Numerical centering and the statistical prior remain separate scientific choices:

\[\begin{split}\mu_{bc} &= H_{bc}\mathbf1+Md, \\ \mu_{bc}-r &= A\mathbf1+Md.\end{split}\]

The anomaly operator \(A\) is signed and has concentration units, whereas \(M\) is dimensionless. Substituting \(A\) for \(M\) would change coefficient units and prior semantics. See Numerical centering for the required accuracy checks and order of arithmetic.

One whole-window correction and state anomalies#

The current additive prior writes

\[\begin{split}d_j &= \delta+u_j, \\ w^\mathsf{T}u &= 0,\qquad w_j>0,\qquad \sum_jw_j=1.\end{split}\]

The scalar \(\delta\) is a constant correction to the boundary product over the entire inversion window. States \(j=(\text{region},\text{period})\) have anomalies that may vary between regions and periods. There is one weighted constraint over the whole window. Individual periods can have nonzero mean anomalies; we do not impose a zero mean within each period. For example, \(u_{rt}=\gamma_t\) gives the same anomaly to every region in period \(t\). It is allowed when \(\sum_t(\sum_rw_{rt})\gamma_t=0\), with individual \(\gamma_t\) potentially nonzero. Because the basis partitions unity, \(\delta\) gives a constant native concentration correction.

The constraint removes the redundant direction internal to this decomposition: without it, a constant could be transferred between \(\delta\) and all \(u_j\) without changing \(d\). It does not guarantee identifiability against site offsets, other boundary patterns or flux contributions after transport. Setting the additive anomaly_scale to 0 retains the scalar common correction and fixes every anomaly to zero, without sampling contrast variables.

Independent priors on the \(K\) corrections \(d_j\) are a valid alternative scientific formulation. We use the mean-and-anomaly formulation to specify uncertainty in the common boundary correction separately from uncertainty in departures from it. The weighted structure gives \(\delta\) its scalar prior and makes it independent of the constrained Gaussian anomalies. Decomposing independent corrections into their mean and departures would generally give a different joint prior. Its covariance and scale interpretation are derived in Anomaly prior and uncertainty. See Configure CO₂ boundary corrections for supported configuration, and Boundary correction structures for per-period contrasts, independent state anomalies, per-period mean shifts and anomaly scaling.

A constant observation offset represents persistent bias in observations; the boundary correction describes bias in the supplied BC product. They enter the model through different components. If \(M\mathbf1=\mathbf1\), both constant coefficients have the same observation-space direction, so the likelihood alone identifies their sum. Their distinct scientific roles do not resolve this exact confounding. Separate prior information or external constraints are needed to distinguish them in that case.

Warning

With the default NESW basis, complete jointly normalized particle weights we know that \(M\mathbf1=\mathbf1\). A global observation offset, or unrestricted site offsets, therefore duplicates the boundary mean’s constant direction. Model construction warns for this combination when the supplied mass response is approximately one. This narrow warning covers normalized surface preparation; it is not a general rank or identifiability test for arbitrary transport. Fixing one site’s offset or constraining site offsets to contrasts removes that one redundancy; BC anomalies can still reproduce site contrasts. See Boundary corrections and observation offsets for examples and the distinction between relative site effects and physical bias attribution.

Anomaly prior and uncertainty#

There are \(K\) labelled region–period boundary states. Define \(\Pi=I-\mathbf{1}w^\mathsf{T}\), where \(\mathbf{1}\) is the length-\(K\) vector of ones. The anomaly prior is

\[\begin{split}u &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,s_{bc}^{2}\Pi\Pi^\mathsf{T}\right), \\ \delta &\sim p_{\delta}, \qquad \delta\ \text{independent of}\ u.\end{split}\]

The finite, nonnegative fixed scale \(s_{bc}\) is anomaly_scale in concentration units. For a positive scale it is the standard deviation of independent Gaussian corrections before subtracting their weighted mean. The implementation uses only \(K-1\) independent coordinates to sample the constrained anomalies. Set anomaly_scale=0 to infer only the common level from mean_prior: all anomalies are exactly zero and no contrast variables are sampled. For a single boundary state, the anomaly is also exactly zero at any accepted scale. Negative and nonfinite scales are rejected.

Centring changes individual anomaly variances and induces correlations:

\[\begin{split}\operatorname{Var}(u_j) &= s_{bc}^{2}\left(1-2w_j+\sum_k w_k^2\right), \\ \operatorname{Var}(u_j-u_k) &= 2s_{bc}^{2},\qquad j\ne k.\end{split}\]

In particular, equal weights give marginal anomaly standard deviation \(s_{bc}\sqrt{1-1/K}\). The scale controls pairwise departures independently of the prior for the common mean; it is not the marginal standard deviation of each anomaly. The variance consequence of a sum-to-zero constraint is also explained in Stan’s marginal-distribution discussion.

This prior keeps the projected baseline \(H_{bc}\mathbf1\) fixed and infers additive concentration corrections. It replaces the multiplicative scaling prior. Choose the common-mean prior and anomaly scale for uncertainty in the supplied boundary product.

Choosing the averaging weights#

The positive weights \(w\) define which mean is assigned to \(\delta\). Uniform state weights define an equal-state average. A physical average requires a declared measure on the native boundary grid. For corrections using basis \(B\), let \(a_\ell\geq0\) be normalized native weights with \(\sum_\ell a_\ell=1\), such as an explicitly specified curtain-area and duration measure. Because the basis partitions unity, choose

\[w=B^\mathsf{T}a.\]

Then \(\sum_jw_j=1\) and

\[a^\mathsf{T}B(\delta\mathbf1+u) =\delta+w^\mathsf{T}u=\delta.\]

Thus the state constraint represents the chosen native-field mean. The current interface requires every state weight to be strictly positive, so the native measure must give each retained correction state positive weight.

The caller supplies these weights; preparation does not infer a native measure or basis geometry. Choosing weights for a mean constraint does not derive the full state prior from a native-field prior. That would also require a native covariance and a justified transformation to state coefficients.

Note

Supplied bases are not checked for partition of unity. If \(B\mathbf1\ne\mathbf1\), the multiplicative baseline is \(P\operatorname{diag}(C^0)B\mathbf1\), and the common additive correction has native pattern \(\delta B\mathbf1\). Also, \(a^\mathsf{T}B\mathbf1\) need not equal one, so the native-mean interpretation above must be reconsidered. The numerical centering identity \(H_{bc}=A+cM\) and shift \(r=cM\mathbf1\) still apply.

What the current surface preparation computes#

We now follow the actual calculations using labelled xarray curtain arrays. The blocks below are pseudocode. The mathematics is general; currently supported centered-input runners are listed in CO₂ model family.

Native curtain products#

The local baseline_reference_sensitivities kernel, called by prepare_surface_site(), matches supplied concentrations to footprint timestamps within declared coverage intervals and converts concentrations and the reference to common units.

require finite supplied concentrations and chosen reference
for each native observation i:
    total = sum(particle_locations[i, direction, cell]
                over every cell on all four curtains)
    require finite, nonnegative particle weights
    require total > 0 and abs(total - 1) <= mass_tolerance

    for each direction and cell:
        mass = particle_locations[i, direction, cell] / total
        anomaly = mass * (supplied_concentration[i, direction, cell] - c)
        retain mass and anomaly on their labelled curtain grids

The resulting arrays are bc_mass_n/e/s/w and bc_anomaly_n/e/s/w, with scalar bc_reference. The products use float64 arithmetic and preserve lazy payloads.

Observation averaging and spatial basis projection#

prepare_surface_site averages the native mass and anomaly products onto the retained observation bins using the provider’s alignment operations. We average the products before spatial projection, using the same alignment for both operands.

bc_sensitivity then contracts them with the supplied spatial basis:

for each retained observation i and spatial basis region k:
    M_spatial[i, k] = sum(mass[i, direction, cell]
                          * spatial_basis[i, direction, cell, k])
    A_spatial[i, k] = sum(anomaly[i, direction, cell]
                          * spatial_basis[i, direction, cell, k])
    # Both sums cover every curtain and its spatial cells.

Without a supplied basis, the four curtains become four regions. Supplied basis values can be fractional. A singleton basis time applies to every observation; multiple basis times must match retained timestamps. The outputs are H_bc_mass and H_bc_anomaly on spatial bc_region and observation axes, plus the unchanged scalar bc_reference.

Temporal state construction#

At model binding, bind() assigns a fitted period from the retained observation timestamp.

for each observation i:
    p_i = 0 if frequency is None else period_containing(observation_time[i])
    for each spatial region k and fitted period p:
        j = state_label(k, p)
        D = 1 if p == p_i else 0
        M[i, j] = M_spatial[i, k] * D
        A[i, j] = A_spatial[i, k] * D

There is one period without a frequency; otherwise calendar-month or fixed-duration rules determine membership. The current mask is binary. It uses observation time rather than a particle’s boundary-exit time. The resulting bc_region MultiIndex has bc_curtain and bc_period levels. bc_period contains each interval’s start timestamp, including the single interval when frequency is omitted. The labels and mask come from one calendar; ordinary nonboundary period indicators retain ordinal labels. Four spatial regions and three periods give \(K=12\) states.

For this implementation we can write the basis in observation-row form. Let \(q\) label a curtain cell, \(S_{iqk}\) be the supplied spatial basis at retained observation \(i\), and \(D_{ip}\) be the binary period mask above. Then

\[B_{iq,(k,p)}=S_{iqk}D_{ip}.\]

Boundary concentrations have already been selected and aligned to native footprint timestamps before observation averaging. This row-wise expression makes the retained observation’s temporal assignment explicit. The native rank discussion concerns independent basis functions over their full domain; it does not require each masked observation slice to have rank \(K\).

A future temporal basis could supply fractional weights. The operator identities would still hold when both operands use the same mapping; the current period interface implements the binary assignment shown above.

The centered preparation handoff explains saving raw spatial operands and binding them to supported periods. The model then uses matrix–vector products of the observation/state operators, centers observations and model means for the likelihood, and restores absolute predictions through the supported trace annotation/replay path.
